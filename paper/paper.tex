% ── Abstract ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
Effective low-level feedback control of small underactuated uncrewed surface
vehicles (USVs), specifically vessels under seven meters with a single thruster
and rudder, requires dynamic models that provide both actionable design insight and
predictive accuracy. Tow-tank testing is impractical at this scale;
parameters must be identified solely from limited field trials. Existing system
identification methods span a wide range of model complexity but offer little
guidance on how much fidelity is enough, because sufficiency is only meaningful
relative to a stated purpose. Absent that specification, the default is a bias
toward more fidelity, since the benefit of an added term is readily quantified
while its costs in identifiability, experimental burden and obscured design
insight are not.

We propose a methodology built around a deliberate spectrum of three model levels,
evaluated on common experimental data: (1) first-order, linear,
single-input single-output (SISO) transfer functions, Nomoto-style models for
surge and yaw that are immediately interpretable and support controller architecture
intuition, but risk oversimplification when considered in isolation;
(2) a physics-based nonlinear state-space formulation incorporating quadratic
hydrodynamic drag; and (3) a Nonlinear AutoRegressive with eXogenous inputs (NARX)
multilayer perceptron. Rather than seeking a single best model, the spectrum is
used collectively: simple models anchor human intuition; structured nonlinear models
expose which terms are dynamically salient; and the black-box model bounds the
benefit of added complexity.

Preliminary results from step-response field experiments demonstrate the approach.
First-order SISO models achieve R2=0.93 (surge, tau=1.95 s) and R2=0.83
(yaw rate, tau=0.47 s), providing direct controller tuning parameters.
Cross-model diagnostics identify quadratic drag as the dominant surge nonlinearity
and reveal that speed-dependent rudder effectiveness, a key coupling for
underactuated control design, is not resolvable from a single short trial,
motivating targeted data collection. A margin-based diagnostic makes the
sufficiency question computable, reporting the forward speed at which the
first-order model stops changing the control design. The NARX achieves one-step
prediction $R^2=0.998$ for speed and $R^2=0.991$ for yaw rate; recursive simulation
falls to $R^2=0.836$ and $R^2=0.768$, respectively, and exhibits surge divergence,
demonstrating that physical structure provides essential regularization under
limited data. High-speed segments are retained for subsequent regime-spanning
analysis.
\end{abstract}

\section{Model Spectrum Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}
\st{OUTLINE} \st{BB}

\begin{outline}
\item Level~1: first-order linear SISO transfer functions for surge and yaw,
  identified as discrete ARX(1,1) by ordinary least squares.
\item Level~2: nonlinear state-space with quadratic drag, identified in two phases so
  that drag is estimated from coasting and thrust gain from powered segments.
\item Level~3: fixed-tap residual NARX multilayer perceptron with one 64-neuron
  tanh hidden layer and state-increment outputs, trained using source-log-grouped
  partitions, with one-step prediction and recursive simulation reported separately.
  It is a compact NARX NN black-box reference.
\item Cross-model diagnostics, four in total: speed-dependent rudder gain; drag
  nonlinearity; rudder-induced surge drag; and whether the added fidelity falls
  inside the robustness margin of the design.
\end{outline}

\section{Experimental Platform and Data Collection}
\label{sec:experiment}
\st{DATA} \st{TBD}

\begin{outline}
\item Vehicle description, autopilot and sensing. Inputs are normalized RC throttle
  and rudder; outputs are GPS speed and IMU yaw rate.
\item Data cleaning: usable manual-mode records are aligned to a 10~Hz grid and
  segmented at mode, signal-freshness and quality discontinuities.
\item Current Level~3 dataset: 44,133 samples in 70 slow-region segments from 65
  source logs. The empirical slow-region rule excludes a complete segment after at
  least 1~s continuously above 3.4028~m/s.
\item Source-log-grouped allocation keeps every segment from one recording in one
  partition. Current training uses 37 logs (40 segments, 28,023 rows); validation
  uses every other viable slow-region recording: 28 separate logs (30 segments,
  16,110 rows).
\item Ten complete high-speed, planing-consistent segments are retained separately
  for exploratory regime-spanning models and are not used in the current Level~3
  paper results. The label is empirical GPS speed over ground, not a verified physical
  planing transition.
\item For direct comparison with Levels~1 and 2, the frozen Level~3 model is also
  evaluated on their original 1,300-row step-response trial. Its 20-sample lag history
  leaves 1,279 scored rows; the existing Level~1 and 2 results use all 1,300 rows.
\end{outline}
